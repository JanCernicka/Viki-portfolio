# Book notes — *How to Create a Portfolio & Get Hired*

**Fig Taylor**, Laurence King Publishing, 2010. ISBN 978-1-85669-672-2.
Subtitle: *A Guide for Graphic Designers and Illustrators*.

**Who the author is (why her advice carries weight):** Fig Taylor is the portfolio consultant at the Association of Illustrators in London, graduated in graphic design from Chelsea School of Art, briefly worked as a graphic designer, then ran her own London illustration agency for 20+ years. So she has sat on *both* sides: she has pitched portfolios and she has received them. Throughout the book she quotes ~50 working art directors, creative directors and commissioners by name and employer — those quotes are the most valuable part, because they are the actual hiring criteria stated by the people who hire.

**Read status:** complete, cover to cover (all 6 chapters + resources + index). Notes below are organised by chapter, then distilled into a scoring rubric at the end for use in Step 3.

> **Important caveat on age — read this before applying anything.** The book is from 2010. Its *technology* advice is partly obsolete (it recommends QuarkXPress, Flash, Dreamweaver, CDs, printed sourcebook advertising, and treats "having a website" as still optional). Its *human* advice — how commissioners think, what makes them bin a portfolio, how to research, write to and follow up with them — has aged very well and is the reason to use this book. Section 8 lists explicitly what to ignore and what to substitute.

---

## 1. Core thesis of the book

The book is deliberately **not** about style. Taylor states this up front: *"this is, first and foremost, a book that's concerned with content."* She assumes you already know your own aesthetic. What she claims graduates get wrong is **relevance and editing** — what to put in and, more importantly, what to leave out, for one specific target.

Her recurring definition of the "C" word, which matters because it reframes the whole book:

> "Commercial" does not mean bland, sold-out or trend-chasing. It means **the work is relevant to the needs of the marketplace at any given time** — if there's demand for what you're doing in your area of expertise, your work is commercial.

The single most repeated idea in the whole book: **a portfolio must be built backwards from one identified target**, not assembled forwards from what you happen to have made.

## 2. Chapter 1 — What is a Portfolio?

### The shift from student to professional (the key transition)

Taylor's central point: **art college lecturers and employers have opposite agendas**, and most graduates fail because they show an academic portfolio to a commercial audience.

| College cares about | Employer cares about |
|---|---|
| Process, growth, experimentation | The finished result |
| Your struggles and failures (they taught you) | Quality, consistency, focus, enthusiasm |
| Development and potential | **Employability** |

> "They don't need to see documentary evidence of the mistakes you learned from. You are, after all, out to inspire confidence not doubt and a patchy portfolio will do the latter."

### What must change after graduation
- Decide which of the areas you touched on during study actually interest you.
- **Weed out anything no longer relevant and replace it with fresh work that is.** This is presented as compulsory, not optional.
- Strike a balance between industry requirements and personal expression. Two failure modes are named:
  - College projects that mimic rigid budget/time constraints can **cramp** a new practitioner's creative flow.
  - Long, deeply personal projects that resemble nothing a real client would commission produce a **repetitive or highly uncommercial** portfolio.
- Employers will forgive lack of experience. They will **not** forgive a selection of wholly inappropriate samples — those "baffle, bore, and irritate them."

### Geography changes what you should show
This is directly relevant to a Trnava/Bratislava target:
- **Rural / small town:** fewer studios, more general in nature → your portfolio must **demonstrate adaptability** across many contexts.
- **City:** more creative outfits, more likely to be specialist → you can and should specialise.
- If you want a specialisation (e.g. packaging, children's books), *"you'll need more than a couple of relevant samples to make this perfectly clear."*

### The graphic design portfolio
The cautionary tale that opens the section: a student torn between advertising and magazine design had **exactly one advertisement and one magazine spread**. Taylor's verdict: *"realistically, she didn't have a hope of landing a job in either."*

Requirements:
- A magazine publisher expects **a sufficient number of spreads**; an ad agency expects a book **crammed with punchy advertising ideas**.
- Research the company, then be **brutally honest about temperament as well as skill** — if fast turnover and crazy deadlines aren't for you, choose book-jacket design over a daily newspaper.
- **Find common ground.** You must be able to say *why they* should hire *you*: shared approach to problem-solving, passion for typography, desire to work for their kind of client, fluency in software they want to expand into.
- You must be able to **talk through the conception, development and resolution of each project succinctly**, identifying how you share their vision.
- **Show how far you can take an idea** — e.g. apply a logo in many contexts *beyond* the usual letterhead and business card.
- Include samples **in a variety of media** where appropriate.
- **A strong personal "house style" is often a weakness, not an asset**, for design employment.

> "More often than not, a 'house style' is a weakness. While personal flavor is fine we are not a 'style studio,' and have to be able to create a multitude of different technical and stylistic solutions for our clients. Diversity is important." — Judy Wellfare, Creative Director, Plus et Plus

> "In publishing, design for purpose is so important in the sense of applying your style to the various genres — for example, thriller or chick lit — that way of thinking is more important to me than a style." — **Richard Ogle, Art Director, Random House** *(the single most on-target quote in the book for a publishing-house application)*

### The illustration portfolio
- Most illustrators are freelance; full-time illustration posts are rare (mainly gift industry, computer games, occasional museum).
- **Style count depends on competition density:**
  - Where illustrators are scarce → many styles help you service more clients.
  - Where illustrators vastly outnumber commissions (London, NY) → **one to three distinctive styles, applied to a wide variety of subjects.**
- Showing too many styles introduces *"the career-cramping element of doubt; it makes the illustrator look capricious and unreliable instead of steady and dependable."*
- The same style can legitimately be applied to narrative, decorative, informative and conceptual subject matter — that's how you show range without showing many styles.

### The cross-disciplinary portfolio
- Being cross-disciplinary ≠ having 25 styles. It means genuinely embracing more than one discipline.
- Commissioners are **as likely to be confused by you as to welcome you.** Pre-presentation research dictates the bias and running order of what you show.
- Some employers love it (flexibility); others prefer specialists and hire freelance specialists as needed.
- Solution: more than one portfolio, or **one portfolio divided into clearly separated sections**.

> "Are they able to rationalize the decision for presenting a cross-disciplinary body of work? Are they able to tie the various areas together in a cohesive whole? I think most young designers can improve their marketability by having a broad set of skills — assuming it is not watered down." — Doug Powell, Creative Director

### Also stated in Ch.1
- **A website should be considered an extension of your portfolio.**
- Your **résumé** is a creative problem like any other: *"You won't convince a much-admired art director of your unparalleled typographic genius with a badly spaced, unspellchecked résumé hastily printed out on photocopy paper."*
- Organisation is read as character: *"If a portfolio is totally disorganized and in a real state, I'd worry about the ability of that person to organize themselves and complete a job on time."* — Jonathan Christie, Art Director, Conran Octopus

## 3. Chapter 2 — Making Sense of the Marketplace

Chapter 2 maps the sectors. Below I have **prioritised book publishing and editorial**, since those are the target (*vydavateľstvo*), and compressed the rest.

### 3.1 BOOK PUBLISHING — the target sector

**Hiring climate (encouraging):**
- *"Book publishers are generally happy to employ recent design graduates and some degree of nurturing is usually possible — downsized art departments notwithstanding — as the pace of book publishing is usually slightly less frenetic than that of the editorial sector."*
- An **internship or published work related to books** will stand you in good stead.
- Junior design positions are advertised in: the creative press, creative-recruitment websites, **the publisher's own website**, and local/national newspapers.
- *"Some publishers will see designers who approach them speculatively, though they may only do so if there is an actual vacancy."*

> "At (entry) level we expect a certain amount of educating to be needed. Budgets and deadlines being what they are, the designer needs to be up to speed as soon as possible, but there is time to develop their understanding of the industry and the language of design." — Richard Ogle, Art Director, Random House

**Prerequisites:**
- **A genuine interest in books and in reading is a prerequisite.** (Stated flatly. This shows up in interview questions and in what you choose to redesign.)
- Think carefully about which areas suit your interests and skills. Concrete tests she gives:
  - Prefer working with photographers over illustrators? → children's picture books are not for you.
  - Would rather read commercial thrillers than literary fiction? → steer clear of literary covers.
- If aiming at mass-market covers, you will **not** work in one genre — and *"a historical bodice-ripper can't be packaged in the same way as a psychological thriller."*
- **It pays to be adaptable.** Book designers do change specialisation (her example: adult fiction jackets → children's book art director).

**Career path:** many freelance book designers **start in mainstream publishing houses**, learn the industry, build contacts, progress to senior design roles, and only then go freelance.

**Sub-sectors and what each demands:**

| Sub-sector | What the portfolio must show |
|---|---|
| **Adult fiction (jackets)** | Versatility; strong typography; ability to use different kinds of visual imagery; **keen awareness of how different genres are packaged**. Jacket design is a condensed process vs. designing a whole book, but turnover is faster. |
| **Adult non-fiction** | Similar skills to picture-book design. Photography is often preferred over illustration. Opportunities exist for *specialised* illustration: botanical, medical, natural history, maps, diagrams, room/garden plans, step-by-step. |
| **Children's books** | Ability to **think sequentially** and understand **visual pace**; innate grasp of which illustrative styles and layouts appeal to which age group; versatility, typography, flair, imagination. Illustrators must show **strong, appealing, above all distinctive characters that stay consistent** (her named failure: the protagonist ages or changes size every page turn). Helps enormously to be able to draw children and babies. |
| **Educational publishing** | Springboard into children's books — **competition is less steep**. Clear legibility paramount. Lower pay, shorter deadlines, tighter and more prescriptive briefs, less recognition — but publishers are *"a little less fussy than their mainstream counterparts."* Not only for children: dictionaries, reference and textbooks for adults widen the stylistic range. |
| **Comics / graphic novels** | Illustrators, not designers. Sequential drawing, page layout, typography and the visual vocabulary of comics. *"This is why editors want to see pages of comics and not merely illustrations of characters."* |

**Hand-lettering is currently an asset in publishing** (stated twice):
> "An illustrator who can combine great image-making with well-crafted typography and hand-lettering can be useful." — Eleanor Crowe, Art Director, The Folio Society

Also: *"illustrators with a strong affinity for type are currently popular with book-jacket commissioners"* and quirky hand-rendered text was in vogue with publishers.

**On hiring the unpublished:**
> "I hire unpublished illustrators once in a while, but they need a great portfolio... The advantages are that you get something really fresh, that's not on anything else out there, and you can also mold them a bit. They tend to be really enthusiastic as well. The problem is that they often don't have their real-world skills yet, so you have to be fairly patient." — Steve Scott, Art Director, Scholastic

### 3.2 EDITORIAL (magazines/newspapers) — adjacent and worth targeting
Relevant because many Slovak *vydavateľstvá* also publish periodicals, and because this is where illustrators get published fastest.

- **Some magazine publishers hire designers straight out of college** and nurture them; others require prior experience (from a fortnight to a year).
- **Internships often lead to full-time employment; some magazines fill junior design roles this way and never advertise them.**
- Practical advice for building the portfolio: *"volunteer your design skills to a local school or charity. Paid or unpaid, if it's in print and shows what you can do, it will count in your portfolio."*
- **Capitalise on every connection.** Keep in touch with anyone who expresses interest — her own first job came because she was the last graduate they had seen when someone suddenly took leave.
- Skills: work to **grids and style sheets**, good eye, layout software.
- **Many magazines make you design a sample spread at interview** to gauge whether you understand the publication's aesthetic. ← plan for this.
- Snobbery abounds and designers get typecast: early choices shape the whole career path. *"the magazine you choose to read may not necessarily be the kind of publication you will enjoy designing."*
- The editorial commissioning process is **very direct** — you show the art editor, who has the budget and is paid to make the publication look good.

> "It's always worth running new developments in your work past art editors. A couple of illustrators I have seen have done this, and I have often liked their new direction more and have commissioned them on the back of that." — Hazel Brown, Deputy Art Editor, Radio Times

> "I would suggest requesting a portfolio review and/or dropping off a portfolio. I would not recommend only targeting the creative director of a magazine as they are often too busy to look at junior designer books. I think that it is better to **target the second in command** as well." — Jennifer Pastore, T: The New York Times Style Magazine ← *tactically important for Step 4/5*

### 3.3 Other sectors (compressed)
- **Design groups:** range from 2-person partnerships to global firms. Specialisms overlap: branding/identity, marketing comms, annual reports, packaging, publishing, exhibition, web. **Design for print still predominates.** Get online and check client lists and staff profiles. Smaller/rural groups need broader repertoires; larger ones may want you to concentrate solely on design.
- **Design internships:** *"some design groups only hire juniors who have done a placement with the company."* Ads for inexperienced juniors are rare.
- **Branding:** requires adaptability, team play, enthusiasm for research, passion for popular visual culture. Susan Williamson's advice is a networking template: *"Do enough research on brands you admire and, working backward, find out who worked on them creatively. Call them up and ask them for work experience. You will almost never see an advertisement for a 'brand designer.'"*
- **Advertising:** networking is *"about the only way to get a foot in the door"*; route in is work placements; target the creative director; illustrators should go via the **art buyer**. Highest fees, hardest hours, briefs leave little room for self-expression.
- **Illustration into design groups:** *"It is not recommended for illustrators to approach a design company before they have published work"* — get published in editorial first, then approach design groups serving the same kind of publication.
- **TV/film, computer games, greetings cards:** covered but not relevant to this target. Note only that greetings cards is one of the **few** sectors where having *many* illustrative styles is a positive, and that computer games is one of the few offering in-house illustration jobs.

## 4. Chapter 3 — Research and Cold-calling

### Why research matters
The more you know before an interview, the better you perform — and some commissioners are actively unpleasant, so preparation is armour. Her own cautionary tale: she travelled miles to a company picked at random from a directory, discovered on arrival it was an industrial design firm (an area she had no training in), and got the useless feedback *"I think you should remount everything in your folder on gray."* Her verdict: *"two-sided time wasting."*

### Learn to distinguish legitimate criticism
She lists what real, actionable criticism sounds like:
- "I'd need to see specific examples of X/Y/Z before I can be sure you're absolutely right for us."
- "Your characters lack the distinctiveness and originality needed for the children's book market."
- "Your X is very strong but your Y needs further development."
- "There are too many illustrative styles here; I find it confusing."
- "Your work is probably better suited to the X sector than ours."
- "We don't really commission much X. Have you thought of approaching so-and-so instead?"

**Rule: if the same criticism keeps recurring, it is a genuine problem, not a matter of taste.**

### How to target
- Investigate placements **while still studying**, if applicable.
- **Scan job advertisements even for jobs you can't apply for** — they reveal company size, location, specialism, sectors served, and whether multi-skilled people are hired, and they tell you which skills you still need to acquire.
- Website first: *"If you want to know... whether a design group handles web-based media in addition to print, or find out if a publisher favors illustrations on its book jackets, the relevant website should be your first port of call."* You may also find job ads, calls for speculative submissions, and internship info there.

**The template for how to research a person (not just a company):**
> "Research the companies you like the work of. Not just the obvious ones. Find out who works in them and who gives out the jobs; his/her name (correct spelling) and a bit about them — their background; college, university. Send them a thoughtful, considered, and personal professional letter/email, well laid out and signed. **No 'Dear anybody...' stock letters.**" — Andy Ewan, Founding Partner, Ewan Searle Studio

### Research resources
Professional journals; annuals and sourcebooks (**check who the judges are** — less famous judges are approached by fewer people); yearbooks, purchasable mailing lists and industry directories; **libraries** (art college and public reference sections) for anyone short of money; industry websites, portals and blogs. Individual practitioner blogs are useful because *"practitioners frequently post information on who is currently hiring them."*

### Cold-calling
- Taylor is emphatic: **secure a face-to-face meeting wherever geographically practicable.** Promotional material gets filed; a meeting keeps you top-of-mind far longer.
- **Follow up every mailing with a phone call**, "no matter how shy or uncomfortable you feel."
- Expect the brush-off that they already have a pool of trusted freelancers. Counter: they still need to stay on top of trends. The required attribute is **polite persistence** — *"as necessary an attribute as a winning portfolio."*

**The channel-quality statistic (worth remembering):**
> "Those who phone have 95 percent done their homework. Those who email are 75 percent relevant. Post is worst; at least 60 percent is inappropriate." — Martin Colyer, Art Director, Reader's Digest

**Interpersonal skills are part of the assessment.** Work samples can't tell an employer how swift, reliable or adaptable you are, or whether they'd enjoy working with you. She cites a graduate called in on the strength of great samples who turned out to be so arrogant that a junior role was "beneath them" — in a small close-knit studio this read as a lack of humility that would poison the team.

**The single worst opener:**
> "My pet peeve is young designers who cold-call me on the phone and ask only, 'Are you hiring?' I will almost always take the time to review the portfolio of a young designer who approaches me in a respectful, candid, thorough manner. But I will never — no matter how hungry I am for new talent — answer 'Are you hiring?' with 'Yes.'" — Doug Powell

### Speculative applications
- Junior vacancies often aren't advertised; those that are usually demand 1–2 years' experience. **So speculative approaches are the norm, not the exception.**
- **Do not blanket-bomb.** Unfocused begging letters to everyone within 100 miles are expensive and demoralising.
- Email is cheap but easily mislaid, caught by spam filters, or resented if the file is large.
- Commissioners are split on digital vs print, but **all agree they hate having their time wasted.** Irrelevant samples get binned and you get no feedback. Misleading samples may get you an interview — one that goes badly.

> "All our juniors come via work placements, people who either keep in touch or make themselves indispensable while they're here. We do see designers who approach us speculatively. If we like a PDF or website, we'll call someone in. **We never advertise.**" — Rob Howsam, Creative Director, Purpose Design

### Exhibitions and competitions
At degree shows **they research you, not you them** — stay near your stand, provide a visitors' book and plenty of take-away promo. Entering competitions raises your profile substantially.

## 5. Chapter 4 — Creating a Good Portfolio ★ (the core chapter for Step 3)

### The governing definition
> "A good portfolio is one that lands the type of job or commission you're looking for."

It should showcase your skills *"without boring your target to death or, conversely, giving them short shrift."*

### The cautionary tale (over-showing)
An established botanical illustrator made her look through **five portfolios**, including ~40 near-identical unbound black-and-white garden plans. *"No commissioner needs that much convincing somebody can draw a water feature."* The fix she prescribes: **three plans, maybe one in colour, showing three discernibly different types of garden design.** Point made in under a minute.

### Time reality — plan for this
- Most introductory presentations do **not** last an hour. *"truth be told, you will be lucky if you get 15 minutes. (And as for 15 uninterrupted minutes...)"*
- **Portfolio drop-offs are the norm** for many organisations → your core strengths must be legible **in your absence**, without your narration.
- Senior people can zip through a portfolio in minutes and still take it all in — visual communication is their job. *"it doesn't take all that long to answer the question: 'Is this person good enough to work for me?'"*

### Learn to be objective
- **Start with your strongest piece and end on a similarly high note.**
- Aim for **~87% satisfaction** with everything you include (her deliberately specific, realistic target).
- **Trust your own judgment over conflicting tutor feedback.** She has repeatedly heard *"So-and-so told me this was my strongest piece of work but I never really liked it."* Including a piece you privately doubt is panic ("but what if it's the piece that gets me the job?") — and her answer is: if it's bad, or you'd hate to repeat the exercise, *would you want the job it got you?*
- **Bad work sticks in the viewer's mind.** Categories of bad: irrelevant, derivative, repetitive, overly self-indulgent, out of date, or simply below the standard of the rest.
- Inconsistency is the specific killer: it suggests you can't tell your good work from your bad, which makes trusting you risky when there are plenty of alternatives.

> "I would rather see a book with only a few really good pieces, than one with a lot of inconsistent pieces." — Steve Scott, Scholastic

> "Seeing work of varying standards in the same portfolio would put me off commissioning someone." — Anamaria Stanley, TimeOut

### The contents — her six diagnostic questions

**Q1. Are all the pieces up to my highest standards?**
Remove anything in these three categories:
- **Work you think is weak.** *"Anything you might feel tempted to make excuses for should not be on display; ditto anything you know another practitioner could do ten times better than you. If you don't believe in it, bin it."*
- **Work that's misleading:** one-off experiments you couldn't replicate; college exercises that failed to engage you; jobs taken purely for money; styles you no longer enjoy (or never did). *"They'll only assume you want to do more of it."*
- **Work you can't deliver:** techniques needing equipment you no longer have access to (e.g. college printmaking facilities — but clients need work at weekends and over summer), or that took ten times longer than a real deadline allows.

**Q2. Is self-indulgent work taking up prime portfolio space?**
- Personal projects are ideal for conveying individuality, **but the work must be accessible to get you hired.**
- Blunt: rough half-finished observational sketchbook drawings of *"a) your grandma, b) your pet, c) random people in cafés, or d) jazz musicians"* **are not illustration** and have no business taking up portfolio space. *"Most clients take it for granted that an illustrator knows how to draw."*
- **The self-directed final major project is the classic problem** — it often dominates a graduate portfolio. If it has commercial relevance it's a good foundation; if it's self-indulgent and hard for clients to relate to commercially, *"you will in all probability have to create a new one."*

**Q3. What new samples should I add?**
- The rule: **"Advertising agencies want to see advertisements; book publishers want to see books, and so on."**
- Then **demonstrate versatility inside that sector.** Her explicit prescription for publishing:
  > "show a book publisher cover designs that span a variety of genres; also give them double-page spreads if and where applicable; and consider promotional posters. Strive to reflect an understanding of the readership the company targets and an appreciation of the kind of literature they publish."
- You may need a broad body of work and then **swap pieces in and out depending on who you're seeing.**
- For broad-based studios, show both range of problems solved **and consistency of vision.**
- Take one identity further: apply it to interiors, exteriors, website, future packaging — *"show how it works beyond the standard letterhead."*
- **"A portfolio is rarely 'done' for the duration; consider it a work in progress."**

**Q4. Should new samples be put in context?**
- For designers, **the work is the context**.
- For illustrators, the **mock-up** — replacing the illustration in a real published layout with your own — is the easiest way to persuade a commissioner of your usability, and is a genuinely useful exercise (you must respond to an uninspiring text, work to a set size, cope with unsympathetic typography, and see your work reduced).
- **Mock-ups are legitimate** — you're not passing off anyone's work as your own.
- Mock-up rules:
  - Choose simply designed, uncramped layouts.
  - Choose layouts where the image is large enough to do your work justice.
  - **Always use the article you actually illustrated** (a stock-market drawing next to a summer pudding recipe won't work).
  - Display **only your amended version**, not the original beside yours.
  - No old, dusty mock-ups. If an old idea was great, rework it.
  - Avoid systematically pairing a mock-up with a larger version of the same artwork opposite — repetitious.
- If no mock-ups or published samples: **use captions** — legible, concise, consistent in style, discreet, never dominating the work.
- Warning for designers: if you're applying for a **design** job, don't let illustration/photography overshadow the design content — especially in editorial, where roles are strictly defined.

**Q5. How many samples?**
- No one-size-fits-all; it's down to discretion and common sense. Some art directors say **3–6 projects**; she notes that's fine for branding but *"scarcely enough to show their full range if they want to design book jackets."*
- **Leave nothing to chance.** If you specialise in animals but only show cats and dogs, a well-served commissioner will assume you can't draw guinea pigs or zebras. *"Don't expect potential employers to be mind-readers; some of them will require you to spell out the obvious."*

**Q6. How do I show published work?**
- **Give it pride of place — as near the front as possible.** For an interviewer nervous about a novice, it marks you as professional immediately.
- **Freelance work counts as professional experience** — including jobs for relatives and friends, live college briefs, and internship projects. *"Your interviewer won't know you designed that logo for your brother's friend's record label... to them it's simply evidence that someone was prepared to take a chance on you, and you delivered."*
- **Do not use chronological order.** That's a college habit; in a real presentation the viewer may lose attention before reaching your best work.
- Illustration commissioners generally like to see work **in its printed context**; advertising art directors prefer images **in isolation** (they're critical of rivals' campaigns).

### Sketchbooks
**Yes — include one or two, as an optional extra at the end** of the presentation. Opinions differ and viewers bore easily, so: don't include developmental work for every project; concentrate on one project, or the ones that show your thinking at its best. *"Too many roughs could make you seem unfocused."*

### How many portfolios?
- Vastly different styles for separate markets (her example: biting political satire vs. picture books for under-fives) → **separate portfolios**.
- Otherwise one portfolio with a **logical flow**, grouped by subject, medium or mood. It must read as a **cohesive whole**: *"A jumble of random images that bear little relation to each other is unlikely to be remembered as anything other than a mess — and the chances are it won't even be remembered."*
- Consider **two versions: one for drop-offs, one for face-to-face.**

## 6. Chapter 5 — Choosing the Right Portfolio (format & craft)

### Print
- Print is *"just about bombproof"*: fast to view, passable around a meeting, scannable/photocopiable, works for drop-offs, no batteries, no tech failure, cheaper than a laptop.
- **Size: ANSI B (≈A3) and ANSI A (≈A4) are the industry norm** — because you usually present in the target's own cramped workspace, and a portfolio small enough to sit in their lap is appreciated.
- She personally favours **A3 over A4 when showing published work in context**, because a double-page spread or wraparound jacket fits on a single page instead of being sliced in half or halved in size. Sets of covers/packaging look more impressive at A3; delicate or detailed work becomes hard to read at A4.
- **Show good-quality digital printouts rather than precious originals** (loss/damage risk — she once left a portfolio on a train).

### Format types
- **Bound:** keeps everything together, nothing goes astray in a drop-off, quick to view. **Choose a system where leaves can be added/removed** — otherwise you're stuck with empty leaves or paying to change the running order.
- **Box:** lets the interviewer handle individual pieces (graphic design is meant to be touched) — but only viable if you can reproduce a sample once it gets worn; for published one-offs, get high-res images made instead.
- Covers don't have to be black leather (she's seen denim, Day-Glo PVC, AstroTurf) — *"just don't lose sight of the fact that it's what's inside the portfolio that counts."*

### Craft rules for display (concrete and checkable)
- **Always protect work** — leaves, or laminate loose samples.
- **Mount colour must flatter, not dominate.** Dark gothic work on black = invisible; pale ethereal work on white = invisible. Forget any college rule about black/white/grey.
- **No mounts within mounts within mounts.**
- **Avoid bulky mounting board** — it makes the folder weigh a ton and splits the eyelets of ring-bound leaves. Use good-quality paper or lightweight card.
- Any descriptive text should be **discreetly printed** on the mount. *"sticky labels and handwritten Post-Its look amateurish."*
- **Don't leave facing pages blank** — two pieces can coexist on a spread without cancelling each other out. *"If they can't, the chances are one of them wasn't all that good to begin with."*
- **Decide one orientation for portrait and landscape samples and stick to it** throughout, to minimise folder-twirling.
- **Photographs of 3-D/large work must be properly shot** — tripod, decent camera or a photographer. *"cheap, out-of-focus snapshots of illustrators' work leaning against a garden fence on an overcast day — and there's simply no excuse for this kind of unprofessionalism."*

> "They should be mounted in sleeves. It illustrates attention to detail and a better presentation to me... a jumble of work in a case doesn't look professional or organized." — Richard Ogle, Random House

### Digital
- Necessary for motion graphics and web; some commissioners actively prefer a website or PDF **before or instead of** meeting.
- Advantages: bookmarkable, viewable at leisure with no hard sell, easily shared with colleagues or forwarded to clients anywhere.
- Laptop for face-to-face: light, small desk footprint, flexible.
- Downsides: can't be left for a drop-off; no tactile handling.
- **What goes wrong** (all observed by her): unfamiliar borrowed laptops; a screen literally hanging off; *"But it must be here! I transferred the file last night!"*; arriving with a CD/stick assuming they'll use the host's computer; assuming Wi-Fi exists; type/images rendering wrongly; and desktops full of unsorted random files — *"the digital equivalent of upending the loose, unsorted contents of a large student portfolio."*
- **How to get it right:** an overall plan and a **logical running order** decided in advance; highest-quality images, quick to access; **categorised, clearly labelled files** so you can answer a request instantly (*"Clicking on a succession of mystery JPGs is less likely to inspire confidence"*); full charge plus cables and power adaptor; memory stick backup in case the server is down; some printed samples; mobile broadband if you have it. **Never assume you can use their computer.**
- Remote: check what format is welcome; tailor it; don't bombard; avoid huge files.

## 7. Chapter 6 — Making Yourself Known in the Marketplace

### Networking
- The main argument for meetings: even if your contact has no vacancy, **they may know someone who does** — and personal recommendation beats cold-calling decisively.
- **Always ask, after positive feedback: "do you know anyone else who might like my work?"**
- **Don't dismiss junior contacts** — creatives progress fast, and in some places juniors get the freshest projects. *"Meeting a future art director/editor could give you precisely the break you need."*

> "I need to get great samples **or a recommendation from someone I know** before I will meet. With either of those, I'm happy to meet." — Steve Scott, Scholastic

**Her own case study (the tactic she personally used and endorses):** she wrote to firms saying she realised they had no jobs going, but was sure she'd benefit from their professional opinion, and asked for a few minutes of their time. She admits the framing was a pretext — the real hope was an unpublicised vacancy — *"and it turned out that quite a few of them had."* Result: shortlisted twice, then a month-long paid-in-cash internship, then another job offer plus a 3-month internship at a top London branding consultancy, and finally a full-time job through the college grapevine. Her conclusion: **"Networking can be hard graft but, eventually, it pays off."**

### Pitching for a meeting
- Phone: to get past a PA, give **just your first name, casually** — there's a good chance they'll assume you're a friend or relative and put you through. (Presented as unorthodox but effective.)
- Failing that, **attend industry events** — award ceremonies, private views, professional seminars — with the express purpose of meeting elusive targets.
- **For speculative approaches she advises a letter over email.** Requirements:
  - Grammatically perfect, **not a single typo** — *"if necessary, ask someone who speaks your target's language to check the letter before you send it"* ← relevant if writing in a second language.
  - **Addressed to a named contact.** *"Dear Sir/Madam"* or *"To Whom It May Concern"* gets binned instantly.
  - Polite, focused, succinct — **never more than one side of A4.**
  - Must make clear **you know what they do** and how your skills are relevant.
  - Her worked example for exactly this target: *"I was interested in your striking repackaging of so-and-so's backlist for Penguin. As you can see from the attached samples, which formed part of my final major project prior to graduation, literary fiction is one of my main interests as a designer."*
  - Paper, typeface, layout and **letterhead** should reflect your creative persona — *"A memorable, well-designed letterhead can be as effective in engaging their attention as more complex projects in your portfolio."*

### Résumé / CV
- **Designers: include one.** Max **two sides of A4**, reverse chronological, and *"as visually representative of the way you think and communicate as the rest of your portfolio."* Employers expect solid evidence of typographic skill and personal flair.
- **Illustrators: a résumé is "as much use... as the proverbial chocolate teapot"** — put notable clients in the covering letter instead and concentrate on strong samples.
- The two poles of opinion, both worth knowing:
  > "For me the résumé is the most important piece of promotional material. The number of dreadful résumés I see is staggering. **If they can't design a résumé they probably can't design.**" — Colin McHenry, Centaur Media

  > "I never look at résumés; only the work interests me." — Martin Colyer, Reader's Digest
- She reproduces a model student letter+CV (Millie Scarlett Davies → Pentagram) and praises it as *"focused, attractively branded, and — at one sheet long — succinct... articulate, non-generic, and shows she's done her homework."* Its structure: names a **specific project** of theirs and says precisely what she admired about it → her own relevant recent experience → what she's working on and what she likes → offers a **gift/leave-behind** → asks for **feedback** and a studio visit → thanks.
- Also: some employers ask for extras. *"I also ask for three samples of things candidates are inspired by. On one occasion I have hired someone on the spot because of what she brought."* — Josh Silverman

### Promotional material
- Purpose: **introduce** before a meeting, or **remind** afterwards.
- **Avoid overkill.** One general-purpose piece **plus one or two carefully selected best samples**, beautifully printed on good stock, beats sending your whole portfolio. Sending everything gives them the perfect excuse not to meet you: *"we've got a pretty good idea of your range here, so we'll just keep it on file."* — *"Your meeting is strangled at birth."*
- **Novelty items belong as leave-behinds after a meeting**, not as introductions — opinion is sharply divided and some find gimmicks seriously annoying.
- **Every promotional sample must carry your name and contact details**, print or digital.
- Print beats CDs; she strongly advises against CDs unless requested.
- Gimmicks are high-risk/high-reward. Her two advertising anecdotes: the portfolio containing **raw fish** ("Do you have a plaice for us?") that decomposed and stank out the agency for months — versus the two hopefuls who wrote in the persona of a woman claiming a 22-year-old dalliance had produced twin sons now wanting to work in advertising, which **worked** and got them hired.

> "I don't think there needs to be a huge promotional gimmick. It is much more important to focus on a strong, consistent portfolio." — Jennifer Pastore

> "Sometimes the promotion work outweighs the actual work samples. It's important that the effort isn't placed in the promotion and that the work doesn't suffer to support this." — Kristina DiMatteo

### Presentation
**Dress:** most creative employers dress like slightly upmarket versions of their student selves. Dress in a way that makes you feel confident, smart (by your own definition) and physically comfortable. Dress more formally only for corporate/financial clients.

**The three rules:**
1. **Be punctual.** If seriously early, kill time in a café; if late, expect a cool reception. Arriving on time signals reliability, *"of paramount importance to employers."*
2. **Make and maintain friendly eye contact.** She taught a career-management course where students role-played interviews and *"Many of them avoided all eye contact; instead they stared into space or at the floor... They couldn't have communicated less."*
3. **Show you've done your homework.**

**What to bring:** the address and phone number of your destination plus a map; your phone; extra copies of your résumé, business cards and promo material (*"You may be introduced to more contacts than you expected"*); a diary, notebook and working pen (what if you're asked to take a brief or schedule a second interview?); and full digital backup.

**How to talk about the work:**
- Be able to **define the problem each project posed, explain how you solved it, and why you chose that solution.** Marshalling these facts beforehand is the surest confidence boost.
- Freelancers note: **non-creative clients care about *what* you did, not *how* you got there.**
- They are not looking for hard sell. They want **logical thinking, creative understanding, talent, and a personality they can work with** — plus honesty, including the ability to admit weaknesses.
- **Take your cue from the interviewer on how much to talk.** Her story: a publishing art director who wouldn't laugh at her anecdotes or respond at all — she wrote the meeting off as a disaster; a week later he called with two book jackets perfectly matched to two illustrators he'd seen. *"An unwillingness to chat can simply indicate intense concentration."*
- Her recommended approach: **offer to say a little about each sample, invite them to ask for more, then follow their lead.**

> "Don't fill your time at an interview by talking. Keep quiet unless answering a question. You don't want to create an opportunity to say the wrong thing. **Sometimes the silence at an interview is a test.**" — Kristina DiMatteo, Print

> "It is a very small industry, everyone knows everyone, and so while ambition is a good attribute, **modesty and honesty will get you much farther than bullshitting.** It is also really important to give accurate credit to the teams and companies that you have worked or collaborated with." — Judy Wellfare

> "A lot of it, over and above talent, is down to personality. If someone is talented but annoying, we won't employ them. Conversely, if someone is getting there with room for improvement, but open and willing, we won't rule them out." — Anne Brassier, Airside

> "If a student or designer has work in the portfolio, it should be their work and they should be able to talk about whatever role they played in it. If it was designed by a senior designer and they helped with the final, that's OK, **just be honest about your role.**" — Robert Linsky

### After the meeting
- **Send a thank-you note.** Email is fine once you've met — *"particularly if they liked your work."* And it gives you a **legitimate excuse to send a few more samples.**
- **Manufacture future contact points:** a change of address or new website, an award or competition win, inclusion in an annual, a fresh promotional piece, spare printer's proofs. (Her example: a pile of spare dust jackets sent out as reminders cost almost nothing in postage and yielded a lucrative job within days.)
- **Follow-up visits matter but must not feel like harassment.** When someone says keep in touch, **ask when it would be acceptable to contact them again** — answers range from "end of next week" to "this time next year" — then **note it in your diary and do exactly that**, even if a promised job never happened.
- You **don't** need to replace every sample before a follow-up — they won't remember them all, and may not even have noticed a piece that later excites them. But you **must** act on any specific recommendation they made (drop this, improve that, come back when you have published work).

### Website
- Recommended for **all** practitioners; mandatory for web designers.
- **Navigation should be self-evident; images viewable at a generous size; fast loading; tested in the browsers your contacts use; type legible and free of typos.**
- If building one terrifies you, hiring a web-design student from a local art college is *"a cost-effective and mutually beneficial solution."*

> "Websites can be created so easily there isn't really any excuse for an illustrator not to have one — the simpler the better... With the invention of the Internet there aren't any boundaries any more." — Dave Day, Fallon

> "A website link can be entered easily into our employment database. CDs get lost in closets, paper gets swallowed by paper." — Judy Wellfare

## 8. What is out of date (2010) — and the modern substitute

Applying these uncritically would look dated to a Slovak publisher in 2026. Explicit list:

| Book says | Status | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| QuarkXPress as a core skill | **Obsolete** | Adobe InDesign (still king in publishing), plus Illustrator/Photoshop |
| Flash / Dreamweaver / HTML-fallback version | **Obsolete** | Figma for UI; plain responsive HTML/CSS |
| CDs, DVDs, memory sticks as delivery | **Obsolete** | A URL. One link to a live portfolio site + a PDF attachment |
| Printed sourcebook advertising | **Obsolete/luxury** | Behance, Instagram, LinkedIn, personal domain |
| "A website may not yet be considered an absolute necessity" | **Reversed** | A personal site is now the baseline, not a differentiator |
| Posting physical letters as the default speculative channel | **Partly dated** | Email is now the norm — but her *content* rules (named contact, one page, specific praise, evidence of research) transfer exactly. A physical letter is now so rare it can itself be the gimmick. |
| MySpace / Flickr / Photobucket | **Obsolete** | Instagram, Behance, LinkedIn |
| Fax numbers in the Resources section | **Obsolete** | — |
| UK/US professional bodies (AIGA, D&AD, AOI, Society of Illustrators) | **Real but wrong geography** | Slovak/Czech equivalents to be researched in Step 4 |

**What has *not* dated at all**, and is the reason to use this book: target one employer and build backwards; ruthless editing; consistency over volume; published work near the front; named-contact letters showing specific research; polite persistence and diarised follow-up; asking for referrals; interpersonal skills as half the assessment; and the fact that junior roles are frequently never advertised.

## 9. Distilled scoring rubric — to be applied in Step 3

This is the checklist I will grade the existing portfolio site against. Each item is directly traceable to the book.

**A. Targeting (Ch.1, 3, 4)**
1. Is there one identified target sector, visible within seconds? (Publishing, not "everything".)
2. Does the work shown match what *that* sector commissions? ("Book publishers want to see books.")
3. Is irrelevant work removed rather than merely deprioritised?
4. Is there enough of the target work to prove commitment — not "one ad and one spread"?
5. Does it show versatility *within* the target (multiple genres/formats), not across unrelated sectors?

**B. Editing and consistency (Ch.4)**
6. Is every piece at ~87% satisfaction or better? Any piece you'd make excuses for?
7. Any pieces of visibly varying standard sitting side by side?
8. Any misleading work (unrepeatable one-offs, styles no longer wanted, work you can't deliver at professional speed)?
9. Is self-indulgent / final-major-project work occupying prime space?
10. Are raw sketchbook studies masquerading as finished work?

**C. Ordering and legibility without narration (Ch.4)**
11. Does it open with the strongest piece and close on a high?
12. Is published/real-client work at the front, clearly marked as real?
13. Is it chronological (bad) or curated by strength/relevance (good)?
14. Can a stranger understand each project in 15 seconds without you talking? (Drop-off test.)
15. Does the whole read as a cohesive body, or as a jumble?

**D. Context and captioning (Ch.4)**
16. Is each project placed in context — mock-up, in-situ photo, or a concise caption?
17. Are captions legible, concise, consistent, discreet?
18. For each project: is the **problem → solution → why** stated, however briefly?
19. Is her role in collaborative work stated honestly?

**E. Craft of presentation (Ch.5)**
20. Are backgrounds flattering rather than swallowing the work?
21. Are images high-resolution, properly photographed, correctly cropped?
22. Is the running order/orientation consistent?
23. Is anything sloppy — *"Any kind of sloppiness in the craft of the portfolio is unacceptable."*

**F. The site as portfolio + the surrounding apparatus (Ch.1, 6)**
24. Is navigation self-evident, images generous, load fast, zero typos? (Slovak diacritics included.)
25. Is there a CV that is itself a piece of typographic design, ≤2 sides?
26. Is there a letterhead / consistent personal brand identity?
27. Are contact details on every promotional artefact?
28. Is there a downloadable/sendable PDF version for drop-offs?
29. Is there a short, general-purpose promo piece (not the entire portfolio) to attach to a first email?

## 10. Immediate implications for the *vydavateľstvo* goal (preview of Step 3)

Recorded now while fresh; the full audit follows in Step 3.

1. **The sector is unusually welcoming to juniors.** Book publishing is one of the few sectors in the book where Taylor says publishers are *"generally happy to employ recent design graduates"* and that nurturing is possible because the pace is less frenetic than editorial. This is a genuinely good target for a recent graduate.
2. **The portfolio must contain books.** Cover designs across **several genres**, double-page spreads/typesetting, and possibly promotional posters. This is likely the single largest gap to fill.
3. **Typography is the gatekeeping skill**, not illustration. Repeatedly: versatility, strong typographic skills, awareness of how genres are packaged, ability to work to grids and style sheets.
4. **The hand-lettering / illustration + type combination is an explicit advantage** in publishing (Folio Society, book-jacket commissioners). If that's in her range, it's a differentiator, not a distraction.
5. **Educational publishing is the low-competition entry point** if mainstream houses don't bite — explicitly described as a springboard where publishers are "less fussy".
6. **Expect a practical test.** Editorial employers commonly ask candidates to design a sample spread at interview.
7. **Most junior roles will never be advertised** → Step 4/5 must be built around named individuals and speculative, researched approaches, not job boards.
8. **Target the second-in-command, not only the top creative** (Pastore) — art director / senior designer rather than only the creative director or owner.
9. **Show genuine interest in books and reading.** Stated as a prerequisite; it will surface in interview and should be visible in what she chooses to redesign (ideally real Slovak titles).
10. **Language:** her letters must be flawless. The book's rule about getting a native speaker to check a letter in a second language applies in reverse here — Slovak outreach is native, but any English-language approach (international publishers, LinkedIn) needs checking.
