# Book notes — *Reach Out: The Simple Strategy You Need to Expand Your Network and Increase Your Influence*

**Molly Beck**, McGraw-Hill Education, 2018 (© 2018; written 2017). ISBN 978-1-25-986091-1.

**Who the author is:** Molly Beck is a digital marketer (Hearst, Venmo/PayPal, then consulting for Forbes and Rice University), founder of the podcast site MessyBun.com, and writer of the blog *Smart, Pretty & Awkward*. The book grew out of a Forbes.com "Mentoring Moments" column that went viral.

**Her own honesty about her standing** — worth keeping in mind, because it's actually the book's strength:

> "This book is not written by someone at the very pinnacle of where she wants to be... *Reach Out* is for people who haven't achieved all their goals at any stage of their career . . . yet. As your guide, I'm not where I want to be yet either—but I've gotten a lot closer since I started Reaching Out."

**Read status:** complete, cover to cover (Author's Note, Introduction, 11 chapters, Appendices A & B, Notes, Index).

> **Caveats before applying any of this — read Section 9 first.** Two matter a great deal here: (1) it's a 2017 US book and some tools/platforms have changed or died; (2) far more importantly, **its register is American-casual and would misfire if translated literally into Slovak business correspondence.** Section 9 handles both, and Section 10 reconciles this book against the Fig Taylor book from Step 1, which gives partly conflicting advice on tone.

---

## 1. The core thesis

**"Reaching Out" (RO) = deliberately messaging one new person every workday** to grow your network — using only email and social media, costing nothing but time.

Three ideas hold the whole book up:

1. **"Network" is a noun, not a verb.** Beck resets the word to mean simply *"the people you know."* You build a network *by meeting people*, not by "networking." The book is explicitly **not** about working a room: *"It's not about how to strike up conversations with people at the grocery store, how to throw a networking happy hour, or how to rock an interview. It's simply about how to leverage two tools—time and an internet connection—to expand the number of people you know."*

2. **The power is at the edge of your network, not the centre.** The people most likely to change your career are loose acquaintances, friends-of-friends, and people you knew years ago — not your closest circle.

3. **Frequency beats brilliance.** One RO per weekday ≈ **260 people a year**; even at a 40% response rate that's **~104 new conversations a year**. Consistency also blunts rejection: when five are in flight, no single silence stings.

The arithmetic that motivates the whole system:
> if a candidate is referred by a current employee instead of sending a blind application, **that candidate is hired about two-thirds of the time.**

## 2. Chapter 1 — What you need to know first

### Weak ties beat strong ties
Beck quotes Adam Grant summarising Mark Granovetter's classic study: **people were 58% more likely to get a new job through weak ties than strong ties.** The reason isn't just that we have more weak ties —

> "despite their good intentions, strong ties tend to give us **redundant knowledge**. Our closest contacts tend to know the same people and information as we do. Weak ties travel in different circles and learn different things, so they can offer us more efficient access to novel information."

Three tie types used throughout:
- **Strong ties** — family, close friends, current coworkers. Trustworthy, but their information duplicates yours.
- **Weak ties** — acquaintances, loose contacts. Where new information and opportunity live.
- **Dormant ties** — people you *used* to know well. Beck's favourite: *"A dormant tie provides new information the way a weak tie does but has some of the closeness of a strong tie: the best of both worlds."*

### The "toolbox" argument for variety
> "The hammer is probably the most important tool, but it doesn't make sense to fill your toolbox with 12 hammers."

Build inside your field, but also deliberately outside it — that's what marks you as an expert rather than just a practitioner.

### Two situational notes that apply directly here
- **"I'm still in school" is an advantage, not a handicap.** *"most people feel an affinity toward someone still in school and looking to get started in their field."* Student/recent-graduate status is **non-threatening and non-competitive**, which makes people willing to help. Ask to shadow someone for an afternoon, or to interview them for a "paper."
- **"I live in a tiny town" is not a blocker.** All Reaching Out needs is a computer and time. And if few people in your field are local, Reaching Out beyond your city is *more* valuable, because local contacts are more likely to be strong ties with redundant knowledge. Substitute a phone or video call for coffee.

### It isn't only for job-hunting
Reaching Out also serves: promotion at your current employer, speaking invitations, finding a mentor, *being* a mentor, more friends at work, getting onto a new project, landing clients.

## 3. Chapter 2 — Jump-starting your digital presence

**Do this before your first RO** — *"you don't want to spend all that time crafting the perfect online introduction to someone you admire only to realize that you have the digital equivalent of spinach stuck in your teeth."*

**Step 1 — a personal email address you own forever.** Not a work or school address (you lose the history and everyone's contact details when you leave). Tied to your real name, as short as possible.

**Step 2 — LinkedIn.** Reasons it matters: it ranks highly in Google for your name, and *"One in three professionals in the world has LinkedIn."* Three rules:
- **Be truthful** — anyone can fact-check you.
- **But be forward-looking** — it is legitimate to list what you want to move *into*, not just what you've done. Her example: a salesperson moving into financial writing can list "freelance financial writer" alongside the current title, because **recruiters search by keyword** for the field you want to enter.
- **Ask for recommendations** — 1–2 under each past role. (You don't need them for your current job until you've left.)

Work-arounds she gives for thin experience — directly relevant to a recent graduate:
- You **don't** have to mark a job as paid/unpaid or full/part-time. **List volunteer positions, part-time roles, clubs and societies** (especially officer roles).
- List soft skills alongside hard ones (time management, mentoring, organisation, public speaking).
- Use years rather than months to smooth over gaps ("2014–2015" not "November 2014–March 2015").
- List your degree/major if you're less than 10 years out of school; list relevant coursework.

**Step 3 — at least one more social platform.** Keep the username identical across platforms where possible. Aim for ~50 connections per platform then stop counting: *"The trouble with new accounts is that everybody starts at zero."* Engage a minimum of twice a month.

**Three-day plan for joining any new platform:**
- Day 1 — register, complete the profile fully, set privacy, add a photo.
- Day 2 — revise everything with fresh eyes; keep bio and photo consistent across sites.
- Day 3 — connect with everyone appropriate **in one binge**, not one at a time.

### Blog vs. portfolio website
- A **portfolio/personal website** shows your work and personality beyond LinkedIn and lifts you in search results for your own name. Her stat, from Forbes: **"56% of all hiring managers are more impressed by a candidate's personal website than any other personal branding tool—however, only 7% of job seekers actually have a personal website."**
- Contents to include: bio, photo, résumé, links to projects, a way to get in touch, testimonials/recommendations.
- A **blog** is optional and only worth it if you actually enjoy writing. If you do start one: pick **one defined topic**, and follow the **three-month rule** — write consistently for three months *without telling anyone*, and only promote it on day 91, once you know you'll sustain it.

### "Public Original Thinking" (Jenny Blake) — for people who don't want a blog
Ways to become discoverable without one: post on LinkedIn or Medium monthly; write a short e-book in your area; volunteer to speak for free or run workshops; be a guest on an interview series or podcast; write an industry white paper — *"You will expand your network from the interview and fact-checking process alone, then will have a thoughtful finished product you can send to them as a follow-up."* Or **guest-post on someone else's site**, which borrows their audience instead of starting at zero.

**Chapter prompt:** choose **three adjectives** you want associated with your name; every profile, post and RO should evoke them. (Hers: helpful, knowledgeable, generous.)

## 4. Chapter 3 — Fear, and choosing your Targets

### On fear
The psychotherapist Megan Bruneau, quoted at length, on why this feels awful:
> "The anxiety we feel around Reaching Out to someone we don't know is trying to protect us from pain... If the interaction doesn't go as hoped, we risk feeling rejected, inadequate, embarrassed, ashamed, disappointed, or humiliated, and interpreting that rejection as a 'truth' about ourselves."

And crucially — **a point that matters more in Central Europe than Beck perhaps realises**:
> "Cultural norms can also play into fear around Reaching Out. In some cultures, it's considered rude, disrespectful, and/or inappropriate to approach someone senior or highly regarded. If a person has grown up with the belief that they ought not to 'bother' someone as busy or respected as X... it's going to be far more challenging to Reach Out."

The remedy is exposure: *"make repetition the routine."* Also: **"Forget the Reach Outs that don't work out and move on: over time, only the winners stand out."** (She only discovered years later that one of her early ROs had never been answered — she'd long since stopped caring.)

### Judy's advice — the best short summary of good RO technique in the book
Beck's early Target, Judy Kou Kasper, on why she replied:
> "Keep it short and sweet, conversational but still professional. I'd be much more likely to respond to an email that **sounded like it was actually written to me and not copy/pasted**. Do your research. **Much more can be learned from a few well-written, sincere lines than an overdone resume** where I have to bust out my dictionary. Grow a thick skin. Don't take it personally."

And on what caught her eye: *"Your email was honest and earnest. Hard to say no to that."*

### The Love / Don't Love Career List → SMART goals → Targets
1. Fold a page in half. **Love** on one side, **Don't Love** on the other. List everything about your current professional situation.
2. Draw a line under both; below "Love," brainstorm ways to get more of it; below "Don't Love," ways to fix it. Aim at what's achievable in **~6 months**.
3. Convert into **SMART goals** — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. Goals must be **within your control**.
   - **Good goal:** "Reach Out to eight new potential clients a month." **Bad goal:** "Make more money" (vague).
   - **Good goal:** "Reach Out to strengthen relationships with six coworkers in six months." **Bad goal:** wait for the annual review and hope. — *"Being passive is not a strategy for a successful career."*
4. From the goals, brainstorm **types** of people who could help, then **specific names**. Those are your Targets.

## 5. Chapter 4 — The Gift and the Favor ★ (the mechanical core)

The reciprocity principle: you always open with a **Gift**, because *"It is human nature to want to reciprocate gift giving."*

### Every RO carries at least TWO Gifts: #1 plus one of #2–#5

**Gift #1 — a compliment (mandatory).** It must name what you appreciate *and tie it to your own story*. Her three-rung ladder:
- *Boring:* "I liked your book."
- *Better:* "I liked your book because this specific chapter/paragraph/concept made me think."
- *Best:* "I liked your book because X, Y and Z helped me [describe something you are doing or attempting to do]."

> "figuring out what's special about a person's work and how it changed you. Then figure out how you can communicate that in as short a way as possible."

Tip: set a **Google Alert** on the Target's name or company, so you can compliment genuine news within days of it breaking.

**Gift #2 — an article or book recommendation.** Signals shared interests and that you're current. Caution: don't send something they've obviously already seen; check their social feeds first.

**Gift #3 — something only you have access to, or only you can create.** Examples: a spare ticket to an industry event (must be genuinely free — never invoice them afterwards); an advance copy or behind-the-scenes look; an introduction to someone useful (only after getting that person's permission). Or make something bespoke that shows off your skill. Her strongest case study: a whiteboard-video company made **free videos for a few influential people**; one recipient shared it and made introductions to the UN and Google — *"well over $2 million in revenue"* traced to that one Gift.

**Gift #4 — a press opportunity.** Being featured/interviewed. Most valuable if your audience is comparable to theirs or in a niche they want — but:
> "in all my years of interviewing people... only a handful of times has someone asked me the actual audience size before committing to be interviewed. In general, people are just flattered to be asked to be interviewed. It's always worth an ask, and if they decline, you can ask if they can recommend someone else you can feature."

Keep any interview short, conduct it in whatever format they prefer, and **publish quickly** so the momentum isn't lost.

**Gift #5 — free advice on a skill you have.** Must be explicitly free, no strings. Phrasing she suggests: *"Let me know how I can be most helpful to you, and I will be happy to provide for free. My areas of knowledge that I'd love to share are X, Y, and Z."*

### The Favor — what you ask for
Not every RO needs one. When it has one:
> "The key to a great Favor is to ask a **particular, definable question whose answer cannot be found on Google** and can be answered easily in a paragraph or so via email."

**Bad Favors (named and explained):**
| Ask | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Can I pick your brain?" | Too vague; and insulting to consultants who sell exactly that |
| "What open jobs does your company have?" | It's on the jobs page — look yourself |
| "Can you mentor me?" | Mentorship develops over time; far too much for a first message |
| "How did you get where you are?" | Nobody wants to write their life story to a stranger; it's already on LinkedIn |

Also: **never attach your résumé unless asked.** It looks presumptuous and can trip spam filters on first contact.

**Good Favors (her list):**
- What industry conferences or events are worth the time and money?
- What trends in our field should I be paying attention to?
- What do you wish you'd done to get ahead at my experience level?
- Were there questions you wish you'd asked when interviewing for your current job?
- Are there books you wish you'd read earlier in your career?

### The "Java Note" — three rules about coffee
- **Don't offer to buy coffee as your opening Gift** — *"it's not really a Gift; it's an obligation."* Only propose meeting after several friendly exchanges.
- **You work around their schedule and travel to a place convenient for them.**
- **You pay.**

## 6. Chapter 5 — The four types of RO, and realistic response rates

| Type | What it is | Response rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-RO** | Someone you knew in the past, or know currently but not well (weak/dormant tie) | **~80%** |
| **Follow-Up RO** | Someone you met in person recently | **~60%** |
| **Borrowed Connection RO** | Friend-of-a-friend / warm intro | **Varies** — rises with how close or prestigious the shared contact is |
| **Cool RO** | No connection at all | **~25%** |

**Rules of the mix:**
- **Start with a Re-RO.** Highest chance of a win, which builds the confidence to continue.
- **Cool ROs ≤ 20% of the total** — no more than one per five-day week.
- *"You want to focus on the edge of the network, not all the way in left field."*

### Type 1 — Re-RO
Highest-yield type. Targets recognise your name and you already have a way to contact them. Fun variant: message someone **the day *after* their birthday**, when their inbox has emptied out.

### Type 2 — Follow-Up RO
> "there is simply no point in making the effort to go to in-person events if you don't follow up. Nobody pitches, accepts, and signs a partnership agreement at a cocktail hour... **All the magic happens in the follow-up.**"

**The business-card rule: if you ask for one, you must follow up — regardless of seniority. No excuses.** Send as soon as possible after the event. No card? Get enough detail (first name, job, location) to find them on LinkedIn.

**The three Hs** — a script for opening a conversation in person: **"Hi, I'm ___"** → **"How are you?"** (react to the answer) → **"So how do you know [the Host]?"** Then the **three Ls**: be **L**ighthearted, **L**isten, keep them **L**aughing.

### Type 3 — Borrowed Connection RO
Etiquette, which she is strict about:
- Confirm your contact actually **got permission** before using the address.
- **Put the connector's name in the subject line.**
- If introduced on a thread, **reply to both**, thank the connector, say you're moving them to BCC, then address the Target.
- **Send the connector two emails**: one thanking them for the intro, one afterwards reporting what came of it (positively framed — praising the Target flatters the connector too).
- *"it's a contact, not a contract"* — a vouch doesn't guarantee a reply.

### Type 4 — Cool RO
> "the bigger the fish, the lower the chance of a response."

Her key strategic point: *"If the CEO of J.Crew responds to your email giving you advice on how to break into fashion, that's awesome. But **the head of your local boutique could give you similarly valuable advice and might also have the time and space to offer to let you shadow or intern**."* Focus less on famous people and more on people with the **time and likelihood** of helping.

Two Cool ROs that reliably land well: **writing to an author** whose book you enjoyed (*"Authors hope that readers will connect with their books"*), and **writing to someone quoted in an article you read** — which subtly signals you move in similar circles.

**Five ways to find a Target's contact details:**
1. Their personal website (contact form or address) — Google name + job title + company.
2. LinkedIn — search by job title or company; connect with a personalised note.
3. Twitter/X — check the profile for an address; or scan their post history for a tweeted address.
4. **Get on their newsletter** — replying often lands directly in their personal inbox.
5. Offline as a last resort: post **one** short handwritten note with your card to the company address. *"breaking through can be as simple as sending a handwritten letter to cut through the clutter of electronic mail."* Only ever one — more feels invasive in a way emails don't.

## 7. Chapter 6 — Reaching Out to meet specific goals

### For a job search (most relevant here)
- **The Notecard Method:** make a tick on a notecard for each application, aiming at 100. It shifts your focus onto what you control (applying) rather than what you don't (being hired). She accepted a job at roughly tick 60.
- **The 50% rule:** *"you only need to have 50 percent or more of the job qualifications before you apply. Most people wait to apply until they have 110 percent of the qualifications listed, and this is a mistake. A list of qualifications is usually a **wish list**... You don't want to be overqualified for the job you're applying for—your next position should grow and challenge you."*
- **Re-RO:** former coworkers are the strongest play — *"people tend to feel an affinity toward helping people they used to work with."* Also: **re-contact someone who interviewed you and passed on you** — wait 6+ months, share what you've done since, show you've followed the company, and name a current opening you're qualified for.
- **Borrowed Connection RO:** talk about what you want at *non*-career events too, so people can offer the "oh, you should meet X."
- **Cool RO:** the right use is HR/recruiters/hiring managers — but **only** for a specific role you're qualified for, or a question genuinely not answerable online. Her HR friend's complaint: being asked *"what jobs are open that I'd be qualified for"* — *"a quick way to look like an amateur."*
- **Always close the loop.** Even if you got the job elsewhere, dropped the search, or nothing came of it, report back to anyone who helped. *"it is courteous to let the person know your status and how much the help meant to you."* It also keeps the door open for a future Re-RO.

### For press coverage
- **Never pitch a publication's generic inbox — always a specific named writer** (find them on the masthead, LinkedIn or Twitter).
- **Reference two or three of their pieces from a few months back**, not their newest — *"it just looks lazy to reference their most recent work"*; older references prove you've followed them for a while.
- Keep pitches **short and informal**: *"If you don't say 'Kind regards' in text messages to friends, don't say it in a Reach Out."* ← **Note: this is US-specific advice. See Section 9.**
- She includes a full pitch template (compliment → "I have a story idea in your wheelhouse" → two sentences on the idea → what you can provide → thanks).

### For a mentor
**Never ask "will you be my mentor" up front.** Mentorship is retrospective — it forms through repeated positive contact and you eventually just realise the person *is* your mentor. Ask for a specific piece of advice instead. Also: *"you don't always have to have an offline conversation for someone to be your mentor. Having a thriving back-and-forth email correspondence with someone more experienced in your field means the person is already your mentor whether you have met in person or not."*

**Be strategic about which events you attend** — the Vivian Nunez story: she deliberately attended a panel *because* Beck was on it, spoke for two seconds afterwards, tweeted, then DM'd, then moved to email. This deliberately **converts what would have been a Cool RO into a Follow-Up RO** — a tactic worth stealing outright.

## 8. Chapters 7–11 — The mechanics

### Ch.7 — The eight-step email template
1. **Choose personal vs. work email.** Prefer personal — you keep the thread and the contacts forever. (Exception: when the RO is genuinely part of your job.)
2. **Subject line.** Three formats that work:
   - Something specific they made or did — *"Inspired by Your June 30 Blog Post"*
   - A mutual connection's name — *"From Taylor Smith's Friend"*
   - Something you share — *"Fellow Cleveland Marketer"*
   **Use title case.** Cited study: title case had a **54.3% open rate vs 47.6%** lower case, and a **32.3% reply rate vs 25.7%**.
   **Don'ts:** generic lines ("Hello", "Quick Question", "Looking for Advice"); the word **"urgent"** (you have no relationship — it reads as disrespect for their time); the word **"sorry"** (save apologies for when you mean them, and it will haunt every reply in the thread).
3. **Simple greeting + their name.** Formality scales with distance: for Cool and Borrowed Connection ROs use the **most formal** version of the name (title + surname).
4. **Introduce yourself — the world's shortest bio.** One sentence, first name only (surname is in the signature). Mention only the identity relevant to *this* Target. **The bio may be forward-looking:** *"I'm Max, and I'm a business development manager transitioning into coding."* — *"You are the author of your bio as well as your own PR person."*
5. **Gift** (#1 plus one of #2–5).
6. **Favor**, if any.
7. **Closing + email signature.** Signatures are underrated: *"people click the links in email signatures more often than you'd think."* Include name, title (or the title you're working toward, e.g. "freelance producer"), company if useful, **a link to a recent project**, LinkedIn, and a phone number if your field expects calls.
8. **Proofread, then add the address last.** Read sentence by sentence backwards; check every link; put the recipient in the "To:" field **last** so you can't misfire a half-written email.

**Rules for the body:**
- Nothing too personal, nothing financial.
- **Do not propose meeting times before they've agreed to meet.** *"You're doing the Reaching Out, not the other way around."* If they agree, you fit into their schedule.
- **Never criticise.** *"A Reach Out is never the place for criticism if you're working to build a relationship. It's a place to be nice."*
- **Anticipate their follow-up questions** and pre-answer one or two, to cut the back-and-forth.
- **The voicemail test:** if you couldn't read the whole email aloud as a voicemail, it's too long.
- Watch for **"just," "sorry," "I think," "I feel"** — they undermine your confidence.
- Privacy — the postcard rule: *"Nobody else is supposed to read your postcards, but you'd be a fool if you wrote anything private on one."*

### Ch.8 — Social media ROs
Use social instead of email if **two or more** are true: you're already connected there; they have 50+ connections (so they actually use it); you want to show off your own presence there; their email is hard to find.

Five-step template: greeting + one-line bio → Gift → Favor (if any) → **include your email address** and offer to move it there → closing, check, send.

Also: **like/comment/share their content before you message**, to land on their radar first. And expect Facebook requests from strangers to be declined — don't take it personally, use another channel.

### Ch.9 — Handling responses (and silence)
**Four response types:** (1) thanks for the Gifts; (2) thanks — happy to help; (3) thanks — can't help; (4) silence.

**Three response rules when someone helps: reply within 24 hours, reply kindly, and thank them.** The evidence she cites for gratitude is genuinely striking (Grant & Gino):
> A student who added just eight words — **"Thank you so much! I am really grateful"** — **doubled** the rate at which people helped again, from 32% to 66%. And being thanked by one student raised helping rates for a *different* student from 25% to 55%.

**If it goes offline:**
- Calls: prepare three questions; smile when you pick up; **respect the time slot** (at 25 minutes of a 30-minute call, start wrapping up).
- First meetings: keep your home/work address private and meet in public; browse their public social beforehand for small talk; **bring a notebook and pen** (*"just the act of having your notebook open... shows you believe important information will be shared, which both flatters the Target and elevates the conversation"*); start wrapping at ~85% — *"It is always better to leave a first meeting with the other person wanting more time with you"*; **you pay**; thank-you email within 24 hours.
- **Three signs they want to continue:** they propose another meeting; they promptly send the follow-ups they promised; **they offer to introduce you to someone else** (that means you're entering their inner circle).

**Silence:** if two weeks pass, a reply is unlikely. After **four full weeks**, send **one Nudge Update email** — reply on the original thread with something *new* (an article, an update). *"The follower-uppers in this world, win."* If that gets nothing, move on.

**If your rates are far below the benchmarks, audit in this order:** are your Targets too big? Is the subject line boring/not title case/missing the mutual name? Is the email too long? Is the Gift genuine and personal? **Is the Favor too large, too vague, or Googleable?** (Put a number on it — *"Do you have **one tip** for..."* — to make it feel contained.) Is the tone warm and grateful? Also check you're not asking for free what the Target sells for a living.

**Keeping in touch: about every six months**, replying on the original thread, with a calendar reminder holding the person's name and what to follow up on. The mechanism (Melody Wilding): the **mere exposure effect** — repeated contact increases liking. *"So many people just disappear that you can easily differentiate yourself by simply following up."*
**Note:** keeping-in-touch and Nudge emails **do not count** as your RO for the day.

### Ch.10 — Fitting it into a real week
- **Schedule A — front-load:** one ~45-minute block a week (Friday afternoon or Sunday evening), pick five Targets, find contacts, draft all five, then release one per day.
- **Schedule B — daily:** 8–10 minutes each morning. (What actually eats the time isn't writing — it's **choosing** the Target.)
- **Best send time:** midmorning on a weekday **in the Target's time zone**.
- **Keep a permanent running list of Target ideas** in your phone and a desktop draft. *"Follow your curiosity and, to some extent, your jealousy (because jealousy, when used correctly, can help you see what you want more of in your own life)."*
- **Her list of people to RO to:** someone with a new job or work anniversary; someone who interviewed you before; a podcast host or guest; a blogger; an author (*"If the author is hard to reach, sometimes I go through the acknowledgments to find the editor to write to instead"*); old coworkers; someone quoted in an article; an alumni board member; a leader at your own company; a recruiter elsewhere; **someone in a similar role at a comparable or competing company** — *"people five years ahead of you and five years behind you will make up the bulk of your peer group."*
- **Three trackers** (spreadsheets): the **Basic** tracker (date, name, title/company, channel, RO type, Gift/Favor summary, response date, outcome, notes); the **Career Inspiration** tracker (adds where Targets studied, first job, certifications, location — to reveal the *patterns* in careers you envy); the **Project Help** tracker (adds each person's expertise and what they could help with).

### Ch.11 — Becoming the Target; introducing people; ROIs
- **Make yourself easy to Reach Out to** — visible contact details on every platform. Then be **responsive and joyful**: *"When you are happy someone Reached Out to you, tell the person."*
- **Introducing two people: always double opt-in.** Ask both first, introduce them on one email with a glowing description of each, then invite them to move you to BCC. Handing out someone's address without permission is the option she says to skip.
- **Reach Out Initiative (ROI):** a "book club for Reaching Out" — 2–10 people, 4–8 weeks, one SMART goal each, a shared tracker, accountability buddies, and a celebration at the end.

### Appendix A — how busy people manage inboxes
A "Deal with on Friday" folder; the **two-minute rule** (anything answerable in under two minutes, answer now); batching email to set times so *"other people's agendas"* don't rule the day; starring as a to-do list; checking mail every 30 minutes instead of every 5; answering only in business hours to protect boundaries.

## 9. What needs adapting — dated tech, and (much more important) tone

### 9a. Dated or dead tools (2017 book)
| Book says | Status now | Substitute |
|---|---|---|
| Snapchat as a networking channel | **Effectively dead** for professional use | LinkedIn / Instagram DMs |
| HARO (helpareporter.com) | **Retired** — Cision folded it into "Connectively," which then closed | Qwoted, SourceBottle, Featured, or journalist requests on LinkedIn/X |
| Google AdWords name-targeting trick | Renamed **Google Ads**; gimmicky and costly | Skip |
| Boomerang, assistant.to, Just Not Sorry, Namechk | Some alive, some not — **verify before relying** | Native Gmail scheduling; Grammarly |
| Twitter tactics (AllMyTweets, DMs, public replies) | Platform is now X; culture and reach changed markedly | LinkedIn is now the stronger professional channel, especially in Slovakia |
| "One in three professionals has LinkedIn" | Now higher, and LinkedIn is far more central | Treat LinkedIn as the primary channel, not a secondary one |
| MySpace/Tumblr/Pinterest as networking sites | Irrelevant professionally | Instagram + Behance for a designer |

**What has *not* dated:** the weak-ties argument, the Gift/Favor structure, response-rate expectations, subject-line construction, the gratitude research, the four RO types, the follow-up discipline, the trackers, and the "target lateral people, not famous people" principle. That's the great majority of the book.

### 9b. The register problem — the most important adaptation ⚠
Beck's voice is **American-casual**: first names immediately, exclamation marks, emoji, *"Hey Molly,"* *"Have a great Wednesday :)"*, and her explicit rule *"If you don't say 'Kind regards' in text messages to friends, don't say it in a Reach Out."*

**Translated literally into Slovak this would read as presumptuous, and to an older editor at an established publishing house, possibly as rude.** Slovak professional correspondence differs on points that matter:

- **Vykanie is obligatory** with anyone you don't know. Beck's entire tone assumes instant informality; Slovak requires the formal register until the other person offers otherwise.
- **Titles are used and matter** — *Mgr.*, *Ing.*, *PhDr.*, *doc.*, *prof.* Address people with the title shown on the company's own website.
- **Openings and closings are conventional**: *Dobrý deň, pán/pani [priezvisko]* … *S pozdravom / S úctou*. Beck's "Hi [first name]" → "Thanks!" would be jarring.
- **Emoji and exclamation marks** belong in the second or third exchange at the earliest, if at all.

**The adaptation rule for Step 5:** keep Beck's *structure* (specific compliment → second Gift → one small, precise Favor → short, easy to answer) but deliver it in **formal-but-warm Slovak**. Her advice about *personalisation, brevity and specificity* is entirely transferable; her advice about *informality* is not.

This is also, usefully, the exact discomfort Bruneau names in Chapter 3 — the belief that one "ought not to bother" a senior person. In Slovakia that instinct is real and partly correct about *form*; it is wrong about *substance*. Write formally, but do write.

## 10. Where the two books agree — and where they conflict

Reconciling Fig Taylor (Step 1, UK design industry, 2010) with Molly Beck (2017, US media/tech):

**They agree strongly on:**
| Point | Taylor | Beck |
|---|---|---|
| Research the individual before writing | "Find out who works in them and who gives out the jobs; his/her name (correct spelling)" | Compliment must be specific; Google Alerts |
| Never send a generic circular | "No 'Dear anybody...' stock letters" | "sounded like it was actually written to me and not copy/pasted" |
| Address a **named** person | Binned if "Dear Sir/Madam" | Always a named writer, never a blind inbox |
| Keep it to one page / short | Max one side of A4 | The "voicemail test" |
| Junior roles are often never advertised | "We never advertise" (Howsam) | Referred candidates hired ~⅔ of the time |
| Follow-up is where it's won | Diarise, ask when to make contact again | Nudge Update at 4 weeks; keep in touch every 6 months |
| Target the second-in-command, not the top | Pastore: don't only target the creative director | "the bigger the fish, the lower the chance of a response" |
| Ask for referrals onward | "ask if they know of anyone else who might like your work" | Offering/receiving intros = entering the inner circle |

**They conflict on three things — and Taylor should win each time here:**

1. **Tone.** Beck: breezy and informal. Taylor: *"grammatically perfect, without a single typo... polite, focused, and succinct."* → **Follow Taylor.** Slovak publishing is a formal, traditional sector.
2. **Attachments and samples.** Beck: *"never attach your résumé without being asked."* Taylor: send *"one or two carefully selected samples of your best work, beautifully printed."* → **Split the difference:** no attachment on a cold first email (spam filters, presumption), but **a link to the portfolio site in the signature**, which satisfies Taylor's intent by modern means.
3. **What the first contact is for.** Beck: open a relationship, ask nothing or almost nothing. Taylor: the objective is a **face-to-face portfolio meeting**. → **Use Beck's method to earn Taylor's outcome**: give the Gift first, ask the small Favor, and let the meeting become the second or third step, not the first ask.

## 11. What this means for the *vydavateľstvo* plan (feeds Steps 4 and 5)

1. **Target lateral, not famous.** Not the publishing-house director. The **art director, senior graphic designer, DTP/production lead, or editor** — people with time, and (per Taylor) the second-in-command who actually looks at junior portfolios.
2. **Mix by type, and start with a Re-RO.** Former tutors from her Masmediálna a marketingová komunikácia programme, classmates now working in publishing, MOJESIDLO and SHAPELESAI colleagues, anyone met at a fair. **Cool ROs capped at ~20%.**
3. **Slovak publishing is a small world** — which raises the value of Borrowed Connection ROs and makes reputation-by-referral decisive. It also means Taylor's warning applies doubly: *"everyone knows everyone... modesty and honesty will get you much farther than bullshitting."*
4. **She has real Gifts to give**, which is unusual for a junior:
   - **Gift #3 (something only you can create):** an unsolicited **redesign or cover concept for one of that publisher's actual titles** — the exact move that generated $2M for The Draw Shop. This doubles as the portfolio work Taylor says she's missing.
   - **Gift #5 (free skill):** social-media/visual help — she does this professionally at SHAPELESAI, and Slovak publishers are often thin on social content.
   - **Gift #4 (press):** if she starts even a small "Slovak book cover design" series on Instagram, featuring a designer's work becomes a genuine Gift.
5. **Good Favors for this sector** (small, specific, non-Googleable): *"Which Slovak or Czech design annuals/competitions are actually worth entering?"*; *"What do you wish a junior designer knew about working to a book grid before their first day?"*; *"Do you use freelance designers for covers, and if so what do you look for in a portfolio?"*
6. **Expect a practical test** (Taylor: magazines often ask for a sample spread at interview) and prepare for it in advance.
7. **Book fairs and industry events convert Cool ROs into Follow-Up ROs** — the Nunez tactic. *Bibliotéka* in Bratislava and similar events are the obvious candidates for Step 4 research.
8. **Set up the tracker before writing a single email**, and use the **Career Inspiration** variant — logging where Slovak publishing designers studied and what their first job was will reveal the actual route into the industry far faster than guessing.
9. **Fix the digital presence first** (Beck Ch.2, and it's already half done): the portfolio site exists and is live; LinkedIn needs to be forward-looking with the keywords a publisher would search (*grafický dizajn, sadzba, obálky kníh, InDesign*); volunteer and student work should be listed without flagging it as unpaid.
10. **The 50% rule cuts both ways** — apply for roles she's half-qualified for, and don't disqualify herself from listings asking for 1–2 years' experience.
